## 4. TL;DR

The 2026 tape has been driven by **momentum, not fundamentals** — amplified by leveraged ETFs, a large retail base (~20–25% of US volume), 0DTE options (~half of S&P options vs <5% in 2020), and quant strategies. Inside the AI theme the market is **not discriminating on future return on capital** — "putting everything in the same bucket." Jewell's fundamental test is *return above cost of capital* + *longevity of growth* + *cash flow*, and on that test the picture is unresolved: MAG7 **cash flows have dropped sharply** under capex, and the market can't yet decide whether AI capex is a **late-90s telecom-style overbuild or a cloud-style durable buildout**. JPM economists expect the **AI capex *impulse* to fade from 2027** (spend still grows, but the *rate* slows). Two concrete reads: **memory is the clearest real bottleneck** (Korea memory CEOs quoted 3-year order books → earnings certainty), and **software is oversold** (market pricing terminal value → ~zero, "like the energy names" — she thinks the damage is overestimated; valuable data sets + real end-user use cases are undervalued).

**Valuation framework + the eroding structural-growth premium (Jewell, ~13:00–14:00)**
- Fair value = future earnings × the multiple the market will pay, anchored on earnings growth, return, and the **risk-free rate**. *"~25 bp shifts don't really have a massive change"* — rates matter at regime scale, not at the margin. `[opinion]`
- The load-bearing driver is **"the likelihood of the moat being sustained in the long term"** — longer moat → higher justified multiple; depends on pace of AI structural change **plus government policy + the geopolitical order.** `[opinion]`
- ★ **The structural-growth premium is eroding:** historically long-term buyers paid a **~10–20% premium** for durable high-ROC structural growth; *"we are now seeing more and more short-term investors come in and that has eroded the premium away."* `[opinion]`

**Market-structure shift — why the tape is momentum-driven (Goulder, ~14:20–15:00)**

- Jewell: a *"much larger proportion of the market holding stocks for only a few months on average"*; a bigger cohort playing **near-term catalysts** and **buying the dip** ("if you've only ever seen the market go up, you buy the dip"). `[opinion]`

**Regional AI drivers (Jewell, ~18:00–19:00)**

What it really means is focusing on the earnings that a company is able to deliver over the longer term and trying to look through the day-to-day noise you see in the market. That is actually much more difficult than it might seem because as investors you are by your very nature thinking every day about that mark-to-market. But as long-term investors, you have to focus on the real things that matter. You have to focus on which of the companies you are looking at have got a moat around that earnings. In the first few years of any share price, what you tend to see is that the valuation — the multiple you pay — actually matters more than the earnings. But what you're looking for here is a function not just of the earnings growth but also the return on capital. I know some people don't think this, but not all growth is good growth. If you're a company growing but actually eroding returns at the same time, that is not a good thing from a long-term investment perspective. And that is a challenge with the markets at the moment because the market has been focused on growth rather than the quality of that growth. If a company is growing and returning high returns on that growth — high returns of capital — it can effectively reinvent itself on an ongoing basis. So it's in a really strong virtuous cycle: it generates good growth, reinvests that growth at high returns, and those high returns generate even more growth going forward.

It's a little bit like Trigger's broom in Only Fools and Horses [transcribed "Triggers broom … only falls and horses"], where he said he only had one broom, but when pressed he actually said "I've only had to change the head five times and the stick ten times." What we're looking for is growth where the return is higher than the cost — the return on equity higher than the cost of equity. That is a big thing we'll need to differentiate within the AI theme. The challenge is that the market year-to-date has been really driven by momentum, and momentum has been exacerbated by things like leveraged ETFs. The problem is the market doesn't seem to be discriminating based on future return on capital. We know that not all AI is created equally. So the market is basically putting everything in the same bucket. If you think just a year ago, all tech was outperforming — software, semis, hardware — and yet this year the market has really differentiated between software and hardware, software being a loser and hardware being a winner. I'd love to dig into this, because the perceived winners have been semis, hardware, memory — the segments that outperformed so disproportionately year-to-date. As active investors that provides a real opportunity.

For example, around the large language models — if these are starting to be commoditized, if the consumers of the LLMs are trying to diversify between the models they use, that is a negative for the LLM providers. So they're not data points per se, but case studies of where you're seeing real focus on scarcity versus the opposite — commoditization of what previously had been scarce.

Turning to the perceived losers — software has underperformed. How do you think about differentiating within it? — This again is a really interesting example of where the market has just been indiscriminate. The view on software has been that future earnings potential has been disrupted, and all the names have been put into the same bucket. What the market is basically saying is that the terminal value of these companies is going to be zero, and zero quite soon. The last time in my career I remember saying that is when we were talking about the energy names — and that wasn't the case for the energy names, certainly not in the near term. When it comes to software, the two areas we should focus on are the ones that have valuable data sets and the ones that have a really useful use case for the end consumer. The market at the moment is really not valuing either of those things. It is assuming that if you can utilize any of the models you will do that, rather than using any kind of software interface. I don't think that will be the case — I think the terminal value is a lot longer out than perhaps we think. Similarly, data sets are incredibly valuable, and data collected in a structured way over a very long period is very difficult to replicate quickly. So I think the market is really overestimating the damage being done to those kinds of companies. Now, I don't think we've got enough confidence in the short term to say there is a catalyst for this view to change. But if you are a long-term investor willing to look at these valuations and think, from a valuation perspective these look really interesting — you are potentially getting a company, maybe low growth and low return on capital, but certainly one with earnings potential going forward that isn't currently priced in.

Talking of valuation, what models do you use to determine fair value? — Whenever we're looking at valuation, we're looking at the future earnings and the multiple the market is willing to pay for that. That always goes back to what future earnings growth will be, what the return will be, and what the risk-free rate is. If interest rates increase, all else equal, valuations come down. When interest rates fell close to zero post the financial crisis, the price investors were willing to pay for growth increased significantly; when that reversed a few years ago, we saw a big reversal. But 25-bp shifts don't really have a massive change on the actual valuation of companies. What it really comes down to is the likelihood of the moat being sustained in the long term. That is what is key right now, because the longer any of these moats around the earnings are sustained, the higher the valuation should be — and that depends not just on the pace of structural change within AI but also on government policies and the geopolitical world order. That can be really challenging to get right, but it's something as fundamental investors we try to do. The other thing I'd mention is the premium for structural growth, which historically has been pretty high. Historically you had buyers with a long-term view who care about getting these companies and are willing to almost pay whatever price it takes — so you saw a real premium for structural growth, 10–20% above what might normally be expected for a company with that level of earnings growth, if that growth has high returns. The issue at the moment is that we are now seeing more and more short-term investors come in, and that has eroded the premium away, because there are fewer investors willing to pay an additional premium for structural growth.

It was so interesting to hear you talk about the increase in momentum-exposed investors, because if we look at market structure over the last six years there's been a phenomenal change. If you dissect the main drivers of the increase in volumes, it's really fourfold. First, the retail investor, who in US equities in particular has really shot up in share of volumes post-COVID, now representing 20–25% of volumes daily. Second, shorter-dated options including zero-day-to-expiry options, which now make up about half of S&P 500 options volumes, having been less than 5% of those volumes in 2020. Third, the continued growth in passives and ETFs over many years. Finally, quant strategies — in our prime book we see that quant hedge-fund exposures have grown more than any other hedge-fund category over the last couple of years. Many of those investor types have shorter holding periods and are more trend- or momentum-exposed. So how do you navigate investing in the knowledge of all of those market participants? — What we're seeing is a much larger proportion of the market holding stocks for only a few months on average, according to some of the data we look at. So whilst some investors are looking at longer-term forces, there is a bigger group playing nearer-term catalysts, and a much bigger group with a predisposition to buying the dip. You and I grew up in an environment where we saw the global financial crisis, so we've always got this nervousness around what the market is signaling. But if you've only ever seen the market go up, then you buy the dip. That means more of the momentum trade, and we end up in this cycle which really is a part of the market we've not seen before.

How do you think about the retail investor, because collectively they're a very significant force in volumes globally, particularly in the US and many pockets of Asia, and yet they're a very heterogeneous group — the younger cohort, the older high-net-worth cohort, the buy-the-dip mentality, the longer-term mentality, those who focus on social media and meme stocks and those who don't, and many who focus on longer-term structural themes very similar to institutional investors? — It's really difficult to know. What it does mean, going back to that very first question about being a long-term investor who ignores the noise, is that it makes it more difficult for that group, because you're seeing the market move quite significantly on any given day. We've seen things like the Kospi [transcribed "Cosby"] move up and down by plus and minus 20%. That can be really challenging. It means long-term investors just need to hold their nerve that little bit more when thinking about ignoring the noise. — And your point that if you can truly compound value-accretive growth over multiple years, you as a company have the potential to invest and pivot into the areas with greatest potential, and if your holding period is long enough you as an investor can be exposed to those very companies. — That is 100% correct. Companies with strong earnings growth and high returns on capital will do well over the long term. The two things that aren't always super clear are how long is the long term, and secondly how much day-to-day pain and volatility it will take before that theme comes through of being a long-term winner. That is the challenge of the market we're facing today.

Before we close — you have an expanded remit, now CIO for fundamental equities across Asia as well as Europe, and Asia has had a phenomenal run over the last year. What are your observations on the similarities and differences in investment approach across both? — The similarity is that it is the AI theme that really is playing out. But what we see in Asia is that the dominant growth drivers are a lot of the chip companies, whereas in Europe a lot of them are in power generation — things like cement and concrete and all the elements you need for data centers. So the types of companies that are the real drivers of the market are slightly different. The other key difference is the market participants: the retail participant that is much more of a trend is something we're seeing in Asia, particularly with leveraged ETFs; we haven't really seen much of that within the European market. So the focus on momentum is much greater in Asia than in Europe. But let's bring it back to the key similarity between all the markets globally: long-term, they will all be driven by earnings. That is the 101 of investing and the key thing we always need to remember as we're building our portfolios.
