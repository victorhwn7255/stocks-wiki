**Chris Hussey:** This is The Markets. I'm Chris Hussey and today is Wednesday, July 22nd, and we're here on the Goldman Sachs trading floor with Vinny Lin, co-head of Prime Insights & Analytics within Global Banking & Markets. Vinny, thanks so much for joining us. — Thanks for having me. — We go back a couple decades, but I haven't had you on the show before. "Prime Insights & Analytics" can mean anything — tell us what you do and what part of the firm you're in.

**Lin:** The primary customer base for prime services is hedge funds and capital allocators in the alternative asset management industry. They rely on their prime brokers for a whole suite of services — consulting, capital introduction, financing and securities lending, reporting, and trade settlement and clearing. As their trades settle and become custodied on the Goldman PB accounts, we have unique visibility into those trades and positioning changes on almost a real-time basis. Our team analyzes that data on an aggregate and anonymized basis and distills insights on the biggest themes happening in the hedge fund industry.

**Hussey:** Let's get into the trading activity right now. The momentum trade is front and center — momentum is stocks going up that keep going up, or going down that keep going down — and we've seen a lot of violence in that trade even as the S&P 500 has managed to stay pretty flat. What's going on?

**Lin:** First, the magnitude of the recent roll-down in momentum has been very sharp. Our high-beta momentum basket, coming into this week, was down 32% from its highs, and it's now only up 16% year-to-date after being up more than 60% in June. One level deeper — if we look at just the TMT momentum long/short pair, that basket is down even more, by almost 40% from the highs, which would be the sharpest drawdown we've seen in the past five years. Tech stocks have clearly been ground zero of this momentum unwind, and our data point to significant length reduction by tech-focused hedge funds over the past two months — the magnitude of the recent selling is the largest on our record going back the last 10 years, essentially on par with the episode we saw in the summer of 2024. And it's not just the persistence and magnitude of the selling — it's how the composition, the source of the selling, has been shifting. Within tech, if we go back to the start of June, the selling was initially led by the hyperscalers; in the second half of June it shifted to a lot of the semiconductor stocks globally, memory makers; and if you look at the most recent selling activity, essentially July month-to-date, it's been more led by tech hardware component names and infrastructure solution providers.

**Hussey:** You came out of the research department — you're a fundamental guy as well as a technical guy. Put the fundamentals around that. Is this just investors taking a little froth off a group that by definition, momentum, had done very well? Or is there a fundamental story you're hearing from customers?

**Lin:** In my opinion, AI is among the biggest tech cycles we've ever seen in our lifetime. But the problem is the trade had become very crowded. If you look at our fundamental long/short returns from an alpha perspective, as of early June our data showed that essentially the entirety of their positive alpha returns was driven by the AI infrastructure trade. Secondly, using positioning changes in semiconductors globally as the example — we started this year with global-semis net allocation being 10% (meaning if hedge funds holistically have $100 of net investments in global equities, $10 was allocated to global semis). But that 10% more than doubled, to 20, as high as 24% in June, which was by far the highest level on our record. Where is it today? That positioning has been reduced from 24% to 18% — still far above where we started the year, but clearly a significant reduction from the highs. And it's not just hedge funds who have been de-risking; we've seen significant activity and participation by the retail community as well. And the proliferation of leveraged ETFs also added additional leverage and impact to the system. The second component on momentum specifically — it's not just the severity of the price roll-down; equally important is the volatility of the factor. When we look at the realized momentum-factor volatility over the last three months, it shot up to the highest level in the last 45 years — essentially the highest outside recessions. So the recent de-risking in the AI trade has become as much a risk-management exercise, as managers look to control and reduce overall portfolio volatility. The last thing — along with the de-risking, the last six weeks the overall market has seen a heavy dose of capital-market issuance as well as technical events such as multiple index rebalancings, pension rebalancing, and monthly and quarterly options expirations. In our opinion this is a healthy reset, not a complete loss in fundamental conviction. And in fact, as the momentum factor started to stabilize toward the end of last week, we've started to see buying activity resurfacing again in the last three trading sessions.

**Hussey:** It makes sense — you climb that wall of worry, and what we're really seeing is the wall of worry show up, which gives you another reason to buy it again. You mentioned leverage and the de-risking exercise. There are normal levels of leverage within a hedge fund; sometimes it goes up, sometimes down. Where are we on the leverage and gross-investment side?

**Lin:** If I look specifically at our equity fundamental long/short book — where their exposure levels were six weeks ago, in early June, gross exposure was essentially at a five-year high, so positioning was very full. And even on a net-exposure basis, net exposure had risen significantly to four-year highs as well. After all the de-risking, where is our positioning sitting today? Both gross and net exposures are now down to a 60th-65th percentile versus the last three years — essentially middle of the pack. So an argument can be made that overall positioning has come down to cleaner levels as hedge funds have taken some chips off the table. But by no means is positioning washed out.

**Hussey:** All of this is taking place while hedge funds navigate a much more concentrated market. I wouldn't think anybody would want to pay 2-and-20 for them to invest in the biggest stocks in the world. How are hedge funds navigating the concentration — do we see them dipping into smaller caps?

**Lin:** First, hedge-fund performance has been very solid this year — through the end of June, average hedge-fund performance is up 9%, which would be one of the best first-halves we've seen in the past 20 years, with all hedge-fund strategies printing positive returns year-to-date. And despite the market concentration still being elevated, it's been a very strong environment from an alpha perspective — because even though index-level volatility has been relatively contained, single-stock volatility has been through the roof, which has translated to stock correlations being at multi-year lows. So there's a lot of sector rotation and movement underneath the surface, which has proven to be very fertile ground particularly for single-stock pickers.

**Hussey:** What's the trade?

**Lin:** Buying the dip in the US AI infrastructure equipment names — the reason being valuation is down to almost two-year lows. But because of the increased volatility we talked about, probably a better expression is to do it using a limited-loss structure, like call spreads in the options space.

**Hussey:** I love that — a little juicy. We're going into the biggest week of earnings next week. What are you watching?

**Lin:** Just like every big tech investor, I'm watching megacap-tech / hyperscaler earnings closely. First, the trajectory of their AI capex spend for the rest of this year into next year, and also any incremental progress on translating that capex into incremental revenue and profit growth, at least for a few of the hyperscalers. And as we move out of the earnings cycle, one thing I'm watching closely is whether correlations start picking up again — because even though we've seen a very significant de-risking in the AI trade, simultaneously we've seen a meaningful reduction in the macro hedges people have on. So this is your textbook definition of de-grossing: taking off your AI longs but also reducing your macro hedges. The market is still pricing/implying correlations remaining low, but that could be one of the factors I'm watching closely as we move deeper into summer.

**Hussey:** Vinny Lin — you always put the insight into the analytics that proliferate across the trading floor. Thanks so much. — Thanks, Chris. — That does it for this week's episode of The Markets.
