> Manual English captions (cleaner than Whisper, but numbers still soft — verify at primary). Full walkthrough retained for grep.

Last night, around 7 PM, you pulled out your phone and typed a question — "What should I make for dinner with chicken and rice?" — and about two seconds later a machine wrote you an answer. In those two seconds your question left your phone, traveled hundreds of miles through strands of glass thinner than a human hair, and arrived at a building the size of several football fields that drinks as much electricity as a small city. Inside, your question passed through a machine that costs as much as a house, got translated into pure math, was processed by chips running so hot they're liquid-cooled like a race-car engine, and the answer came back letter by letter before you lowered your thumb. Here's the part that should get your attention as an investor: to make those two seconds possible, the largest companies on Earth are spending this year alone roughly $725 billion on infrastructure — just four companies: Amazon, Microsoft, Google, and Meta. That's more in one year than the inflation-adjusted cost of the entire US interstate highway system. Goldman Sachs projects a total buildout at $7.6 trillion between 2026 and 2031. Jensen Huang called it "the largest infrastructure buildout in human history." So the question this whole video hangs on: where does all the money actually go? I'm Leo, a VC investor. This is educational, not financial advice.

The internet has existed for thirty years — why did nobody need to spend three-quarters of a trillion a year until now? The answer is the difference between a librarian and a writer. Google is a librarian: when you search, it walks into a library it already organized and hands you pages that exist — a lookup, a fraction of a cent. ChatGPT is a writer: there's no answer on a shelf; the model composes your answer from scratch, one word at a time, every single time. Even if a million people ask the same question, it doesn't retrieve, it generates — and generation is expensive: a single ChatGPT query can cost 10 to 100× more compute than a Google search. Multiply by 900 million weekly users. Search retrieves, AI generates, and generation is a manufacturing process. Think of an AI data center as a factory: raw material in (electricity), product out (words).

Three terms. The token — the product; language models read tokens (chunks ~¾ of a word); tokens are the unit of revenue (OpenAI/Anthropic price per million tokens). The flop — one arithmetic operation, the unit of labor; manufacturing one token takes hundreds of billions of them. Training vs inference — training is building the factory (a model has >1 trillion adjusting knobs/parameters; months, tens of thousands of chips, ~$100M+ per frontier model, once per model); inference is running the factory (every ChatGPT query). The misconception I most want to kill: people assume training is where the money goes — wrong. By 2026, roughly ⅔ of all AI compute is inference, because training happens once but inference happens billions of times a day. OpenAI's inference bill alone is projected around $14 billion this year.

Why did this explode after 2022? Scaling laws. Around 2020 OpenAI researchers found that if you make the model bigger, give it more data, and spend more compute, the model gets predictably smarter — nearly a straight line. Scaling law turned intelligence into something you could purchase — it converted AI from a research problem into a capital-expenditure problem, and big companies know how to compete on capex: outspend everyone. For the entire history of Silicon Valley, software was an escape from the physical world — zero marginal cost, infinite copies. AI reversed that: the frontier of software is now poured in concrete, measured in megawatts, and cooled with water. Software became heavy industry, and that changes who makes money.

[The journey of your question — six steps: the trip (radio → fiber at ⅔ light speed, routed hundreds of miles); the front door (API gateway); tokenization; prefill (the model reads your whole prompt in parallel, builds the KV cache — its working memory — that pause before the first word is prefill); decode (the assembly line — one token at a time, a full network pass per word); the trip home. And batching — your question is grouped with hundreds of others on the same chip, the difference between cents and dollars. The whole factory in one sentence: electrons in, tokens out.]

Power. The story of AI in 2026 is not mainly about chips anymore — it's about electricity. A traditional rack draws 5-10kW; Nvidia's flagship AI rack draws 120kW; the next-gen Vera Rubin racks approach 600kW per rack — a 60-100× jump in power density in under a decade. A large AI campus wants a gigawatt or more (≈ a nuclear reactor, ~1M homes). Data centers consume ~4-5% of US electricity going into this boom; credible projections put it at 9-17% by 2030. The grid was built for 1-2%/year growth; AI showed up asking for tens of gigawatts now. Two bottleneck numbers: the interconnection queue is 4-5 years long (this April ~410 GW of large projects waiting, 87% data centers — nearly 5× the entire Texas grid's peak); and the transformer — a large power transformer used to take ~a year to order, now 2.5-4 years with prices up ~80%. You can have your chip in six months; the gray box that powers them, 2029. So you go around the grid — behind-the-meter power.

The players, from most dramatic to most dependable. The nuclear resurrection: in 2024 Microsoft signed a 20-year deal with Constellation Energy to restart Three Mile Island (the undamaged reactor); Constellation is spending ~$1.6B, backed by a $1B federal loan, to bring the 835 MW unit back in H2 2027, and Microsoft buys every megawatt for 20 years. Constellation operates America's largest nuclear fleet (~22 GW), now a ~$90B company; its pair Vistra (37 GW nuclear+gas) rode the same wave. You cannot build a new conventional nuclear plant in America this decade, so existing reactors are irreplaceable. The SMR lottery tickets: Oklo (backed by Sam Altman, >14 GW signed pipeline), NuScale (the only US-certified SMR design) — but these are pre-revenue, first commercial electron ~2030 at earliest; NuScale booked ~$31M revenue against a $356M loss; both stocks down 65-78% from late-2025 peaks. "That's not a business yet; it's an option on the 2030s." The fastest power: fuel cells — Bloom Energy's solid-oxide fuel cells convert natural gas to electricity chemically, parked behind the meter in 12-18 months; the stock nearly quadrupled in 2025 then doubled again in H1, and Bloom announced $7.65B in data-center contracts in a single nine-day stretch. But this July, Hunterbrook (an investigative outlet whose affiliated funds short the stock — weigh accordingly) published a report saying Bloom's marketed $20B backlog is more than 40× its binding contract obligations (vs ~2× for typical peers), and that scaling to its ambitions would consume nearly the entire global supply of scandium, a metal China now requires export licenses for. Bloom formally rejected the claims as false and misleading. The arms dealer through 2030: GE Vernova (GE's power spin-off) makes the giant gas turbines that are realistically the #1 near-term power source for AI, because gas is the only thing you can build at scale before 2030 — GE Vernova's turbine slots are sold out through the end of the decade, backlog ~$163B, and in Q1 2026 alone they booked $2.4B in data-center electrification orders, more than all of 2025; the stock is now a ~$300B company; only real rival Siemens Energy. Between the substation and chips: switchgear, busways, UPS — Vertiv, Schneider Electric, Eaton; Eaton's electrical backlog grew 48% YoY, Vertiv's more than doubled to $15B and it joined the S&P 500 in March. And backup generators from Caterpillar and Cummins — Caterpillar's data-center generator business could triple by 2030. The uncomfortable part: because data centers bid for scarce power, they bid against you — in the PJM market (13 states), data-center demand added over $9B to the latest capacity auction, translating to residential bills rising $16-18/month in parts of Ohio and Maryland. Moratoriums are being proposed — a genuine political risk.

Cooling. Every watt becomes heat; a single flagship AI chip dissipates over 1,000 watts; stack 72 into a rack and you have 120kW of heat. Air worked up to ~30-50kW per rack, but we passed that permanently — water carries heat ~3,000× more effectively than air. Rear-door heat exchangers (a patch); direct-to-chip cooling (the 2026 mainstream — a cold plate on each chip, hoses to a coolant distribution unit); immersion (dunking servers in non-conductive fluid). Nvidia's flagship racks require liquid, not offer it. PUE (power usage effectiveness): old data centers ~2.0, modern liquid-cooled ~1.1. Who gets paid: largely the same names as power — Vertiv is the leader selling both power and cooling gear (revenue +28%/yr at 20% margin). Then the electricals bought their way in: Eaton paid ~$9.5B for Boyd Thermal, Schneider bought Motivair; smaller pure-plays nVent, CoolIT. The liquid-cooling market was ~$5B in 2025, forecast $15-27B by the early 2030s — the clearest picks-and-shovels lane, because heat is heat regardless of who wins.

[Inside the server: Nvidia's GB200 NVL72 — 72 GPUs as one computer, ~1.5 tons, 120kW, ~$3M. The CPU is the head chef (Intel's demoted kingdom); the GPUs are 10,000 line cooks; HBM the countertop; SSD the pantry; the NIC the waiter. Nvidia: last FY $215.9B revenue (+65%, ~$194B data center), 80-86% of the accelerator market, ~75% gross margin (vs Apple ~46%), first $5T company last October, ~7¢ of every S&P 500 dollar. The real moat is CUDA. Bear case: ~40% of revenue from four customers all building their own chips. AMD: Instinct competitive on inference (25-40% better tokens/dollar), ROCm now 90-95% of CUDA, but ~5-7% share. Intel barely in the race. Broadcom the quiet assassin: co-designs Google's TPU, Meta's chip, reportedly OpenAI's; >60% of the custom-chip market, AI revenue +106% last quarter, $73B backlog, sight-line to $100B AI revenue in 2027 — sells both sides of the war. Who builds the racks: Super Micro (+123% revenue, 6-10% GM), Dell ($64B AI orders, $43B backlog, <9% margin), HPE; and the Taiwanese ODMs Foxconn (~40% of world AI racks), Quanta, Wiwynn, Celestica. Nvidia keeps 75-point margins on silicon; the assemblers keep 6-10. Profit lives in whatever is scarce.]

[Networking is ~40-60% of spend per GPU dollar. Scale-up inside the rack is Nvidia's proprietary NVLink; scale-out rack-to-rack is a war — InfiniBand (Nvidia/Mellanox 2019) vs Ethernet; by early 2026 ~⅔ of new AI-cluster networking is Ethernet. Broadcom's Tomahawk chips are inside most high-end Ethernet switches; Arista builds the switches (guiding $11.5B, two customers = 40% of revenue); Cisco the incumbent; Marvell one tier down (custom chips for Amazon/Microsoft + optical DSP leadership); Astera Labs makes retimer chips, 76% gross margins, revenue +93%. Then light: optical transceivers convert electrical to light — Coherent (leader), Lumentum, Innolight (China volume champ), Fabrinet (contract manufacturer), Corning (the glass fiber), Amphenol (connectors); co-packaged optics is the frontier.]

HBM. During decode, the GPU's math cores are often not the bottleneck — the chip must pull parameters and the KV cache from memory faster than the math runs; modern inference is memory-bandwidth-bound. HBM stacks memory vertically (8-12 high), drills thousands of microscopic shafts, and glues the tower next to the GPU — 5-6× the bandwidth at 5-6× the price. Only three companies can make it: SK Hynix (~60%, out-executed by betting on HBM early and shipping each generation first, unlocking Nvidia's next-gen allocation), Samsung (the largest memory company overall, embarrassingly late), Micron (the American champion, sold out its entire 2026 HBM capacity in advance). In May 2026 all three crossed $1 trillion in market value, combined over $4 trillion, ~16× a decade ago. Memory used to be the most brutal commodity business — boom, bust, bankrupt; HBM changed the psychology (sold out a year in advance, priced like a luxury good). The open question is whether that price survives the moment all three finish their capacity expansions at once.

[Storage: closer to the chip = faster and pricier — cache, HBM, DRAM, SSDs, and the spinning hard drive (still unbeatable per terabyte for cold bulk). AI is a data hoarder — training sets, checkpoints, and the output retained forever. Seagate's CEO calls it "the inference inflection." Hard drives are a duopoly — Seagate and Western Digital — plus flash players Kioxia and Solidigm; both drive makers sold out production into 2027, WDC ships 89% of revenue to cloud customers, and consumer hard-drive prices jumped 50% because AI ate the supply.]

Software. Where does the durable advantage live? CUDA — the twenty-year trap: Nvidia spent a decade building a programming platform so scientists could use gaming chips for general math; now it has millions of developers and every framework optimized for it first. When AMD ships a better-spec chip, the customer is comparing chips plus the cost of retraining their entire engineering organization with a $100M training run on the line. "The moat was never the silicon — it's the muscle memory of a million engineers." And the serving engine (vLLM, TensorRT) does three tricks — batching, KV-caching, quantization — for a 3-10× cost reduction from software alone; when OpenAI or Anthropic cuts API prices 80% in a year, it's mostly this layer, not new chips. Token manufacturing costs are collapsing — remember that for the finale, because it cuts both ways. Enterprise adds RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) via vector databases (Pinecone) plus data platforms Databricks and Snowflake.

Follow the money. Demand: the hyperscalers (Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta, Oracle) spending a combined $725B this year; the neoclouds (CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda, Crusoe); the AI labs (OpenAI ~$850B, Anthropic ~$965B on ~$47B annualized revenue, xAI folded into SpaceX — both major labs filed for IPO this June). Costs: Bernstein estimates one gigawatt of AI data center costs ~$35B to build, ~39% chips (the biggest line), which is why Nvidia's gross profit alone is ~30% of total industry cost. And that machine depreciates fast — most operators write chips off over 4-6 years, and skeptics argue even that flatters the accounting since a five-year-old GPU competes against chips 10× better; every year of depreciation life swings billions in reported profit. Now the loop: Nvidia invests billions into CoreWeave, Nebius, OpenAI; those companies buy Nvidia chips — revenue for Nvidia. The hyperscalers sign enormous contracts with the neoclouds — Meta alone committed ~$21B to CoreWeave and reportedly $27B to Nebius, which moves data-center spending off Meta's balance sheet into someone else's debt. The AI labs sign compute deals with hyperscalers (OpenAI with Microsoft and Oracle, Anthropic with Amazon and Google), paid partly with money those hyperscalers invested into them. This is not fraud and it's not new — vendor financing built the railroads and the telephone network. The loop has exactly one opening where fresh money is supposed to enter: end users and enterprises — you paying $20/month, companies paying for APIs and copilots. That is the only exit that isn't recycled capital, and today that corner is the smallest number on the board. The two biggest labs' combined annualized run-rates now top $70 billion — the fastest revenue ramp in software history — but the revenue they'll actually book this calendar year is a fraction of that, set against $725B of annual spend, with OpenAI still expected to lose ~$14B this year. The entire $7 trillion machine is the bet that a small corner grows faster than the big circle spins.

Two seconds, $700 billion a year, the largest infrastructure project our species has ever attempted — so a machine can suggest you make one-pan chicken and rice. Whether that's the most important investment in history or the most expensive, honestly, nobody on Earth knows yet.
